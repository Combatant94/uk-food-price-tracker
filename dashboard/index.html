Which foods really drove the UK's food price rise?

Official ONS price indices and 75,000+ shop-level price quotes, analysed to separate the categories that rose the most from the ones that actually moved the overall bill, with every result checked against published ONS figures.

The rise, the peak and the slowdown

Annual change in prices each month. Food climbed far above overall inflation in 2022–23, peaked at 19.2% in Mar 2023, and is now rising more slowly than prices overall.

Biggest risers are not the biggest drivers

Oils and fats rose the most, but households spend relatively little on them. The categories that moved the overall food bill were everyday staples that take a bigger share of spending.

Price rise since Jan 2021

Share of the 19.2% peak (percentage points)

Milk, cheese & eggs and bread & cereals together contributed 6.9 of the 19.2 points at the peak, while oils and fats, the biggest riser, contributed just 0.86.

Contribution = category weight ÷ food weight × category annual rate, using each year's official ONS weights. Check: the contributions sum to the official food rate within 0.07 points on average.

157 everyday products, priced like for like

Each product is compared in the same shop in January 2021 and January 2025 (21,766 matched shop-product pairs). Comparing different shops each year can badly distort the picture; switch views to see the difference.

ProductMedian price 2021Median price 2025Change (like for like)

Like-for-like change = geometric mean of price changes in the same shops. Median prices summarise the range of shops, so for loosely defined products (e.g. "whole sponge cake") they can move differently from the like-for-like change, which tracks each shop against its own earlier price. Products with fewer than 20 matched shops are excluded. Across all products the typical rise is 33.4%, close to the official ONS food figure of 34.4% for the same period.

Every region felt it

Like-for-like change on the same 138 products priced in all 12 regions, Jan 2021 to Jan 2025.

The gap between regions (about 6 points) is small next to the rise itself, so the squeeze was national.

Chains rose faster than independents

On 39 products sold in both, prices in chains with 10+ outlets rose 30.6% against 15.6% in independent shops.

Independents started much more expensive: a 53.0% premium in 2021, narrowing to 39.1% by 2025. Treat this as a lead, not a firm finding: it rests on only 753 independent-shop matches.

What this means for a food retailer

Staples set the price perception

Dairy and bakery drove the most inflation, so pricing on those everyday lines shapes how expensive a shop feels far more than headline-grabbing rises in smaller categories.

The pressure has eased

Food inflation (1.3%) is now below overall inflation (3.1%). Shoppers have absorbed a 39.8% rise, so the conversation shifts from price rises to value and quality.

Watch the volatile categories

Oils and fats, dairy, and other food products such as sauces and condiments swung the most, with annual rises peaking above 30%, so they need the closest monitoring for cost and promotion planning.

National, not regional

Price rises were similar across the UK, which supports consistent national pricing rather than large regional differences.

How it was built and checked

Python (pandas) pipeline and SQL (MySQL syntax) on three public ONS files: the CPI time series (MM23) and the CPI price quotes for January 2021 and January 2025.

  • Category contributions reconcile with the official food inflation rate to within 0.07 points on average.
  • The like-for-like product estimate (33.4%) sits close to the official ONS food change (34.4%) for the same months.
  • The SQL and Python layers were run side by side and agree to within 0.05 points. That cross-check caught one centrally collected price copied into all 12 regions, which was then excluded.
  • Price quotes for food stopped being published in 2026, when ONS moved groceries to scanner data, so January 2025 is the last full like-for-like snapshot.
